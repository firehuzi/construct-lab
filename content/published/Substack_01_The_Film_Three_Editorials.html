Fault Lines in Singapore ①

The Film, Three Editorials: Why Nostalgia Stings Singapore

The "frozen settlement" has no defense against positive emotional input — and a Chinese film about Teochew remittance letters just proved it.

In May 2026, a Chinese film titled Letters to Grandma (给阿嫲的情书) premiered. The story follows Teochew remittance letters — those slips of paper that carried money and words across the South China Sea. The cast was entirely non-professional. There was no political messaging, no territorial claim, no propaganda angle. In Singapore, Malaysia, and Thailand, Chinese audiences wept. Some remembered their own grandmothers.

Then, over three consecutive days, Singapore's Lianhe Zaobao published editorials. The film, the paper declared, represented "the highest form of united front work." Readers were warned: beware the weaponization of blood-and-soil nostalgia by external forces.

A warm film about family letters had triggered a national security response. To understand why, we must look past the film itself — toward the structural logic beneath Singapore's nation-building. We call it the frozen settlement: an identity-management system designed to freeze every combustible identity into place. And this film struck precisely where that system is most fragile — its near-total inability to process positive emotional input.

I. The Frozen Settlement: Singapore's Survival Invention

Singapore at independence was not a "natural nation." It had no shared language, no shared religion, no shared historical memory. Three major communities — Chinese, Malay, Indian — each retained emotional ties to external "homelands." Under existential pressure, Singapore's elite designed a unique integration formula: rather than forging a thick, organic "Singaporean nation," they would use institutions to freeze every identity powder keg in place.

The core mechanism of this formula is negation. Not a Malay state. Not a Chinese state. Not an ethnic state. Through repeated negation, Singapore built its national subjectivity on the differentiation from "the Other." Specifically, it sustains order through three negations:

Externally: It must prove it is neither "the Third China" nor part of a "Greater Malaysia."
Internally: The CMIO framework (Chinese, Malay, Indian, Others) boxes each community into administrable categories, confining cultural expression within controllable boundaries.
Externally again: It guards against emotional input from each community's "motherland" — whether China, Malaysia, India, or the Middle East.

This model has been extraordinarily effective. For six decades, Singapore has seen no race riots, no separatist movements, no large-scale ethnic conflict. The cost: national identity is built on what Singapore is not, rather than what it is. It is a defensive, negative construction — one with no positive emotional anchor.

II. Nostalgia: The Emotion the Frozen Settlement Cannot Process

The frozen settlement excels at processing negative emotions — fear, anxiety, hatred. It can tell you: without stability, 1969 will happen again; without resisting external influence, you will lose your "Singaporeanness." These warnings work because they activate survival instinct.

But nostalgia is not a negative emotion. It is warm, ambiguous, backward-looking, and positive. It does not threaten survival — it simply reminds people: you are not merely a Singaporean; you are also someone's descendant, a distant relative of some land. The frozen settlement has no outlet for this emotion. Because from its inception, it assumed: to become Singaporean, you must sever the emotional cable to your ancestral homeland.

The Danger of "Letters to Grandma"

There is no political slogan, no territorial claim, no deliberate manipulation. It tells the story of remittance letters — those slips of paper that crossed the sea. Grandma Ye Shurou spent half a lifetime guarding her mailbox, believing the letters came from her husband Zheng Musheng, who had gone to Nanyang. Her grandson Xiaowei searches for him in Thailand, opens archive after archive, knocks on door after door, and discovers: the person who wrote to Grandma for decades was a stranger named Xie Nanzhi.

Yet this story made Singaporean Chinese realize: the "bloodline string" they were told to sever was never truly broken. It had only been silenced by institutional demand. And one film made it sing again in many hearts.

The official reaction — three editorials, the "united front" label — confirms the fragility of the frozen settlement. When a system cannot process a category of emotional input, its only move is to label the input itself as a threat. This is not Singaporean paranoia. It is the logical necessity of the frozen settlement: any positive emotion that might loosen the negative structure must be explained as the conspiracy of an external power.

III. Generational Succession: The Long-Term Cost

Notably, the resonance was strongest not among the Pioneer Generation — those who lived through the survival crises of the 1960s — but among younger Singaporean Chinese. They did not experience the turbulence of the 1960s. They did not participate in the "survival first" nation-building project. They grew up in a prosperous, stable Singapore and began asking "who am I?" rather than merely "how do I survive?"

The Pioneer Generation: Willingly accepted the negative-identity contract, because survival was the first priority.

The Younger Generation: Increasingly find this contract too costly. They want more than material stability — they want cultural legitimacy. They do not want to be told that feeling connected to China is treason.

In the era of globalization, this demand could be temporarily absorbed by a "global citizen" identity. But in an age of deglobalization and resurgent nationalism, it inevitably points toward a rediscovery of blood, history, and culture. Singapore's officials are not blind to this — which is why Lianhe Zaobao's language was so severe. It attempted to redraw an increasingly blurred red line: you are Singaporean, not Chinese. Any emotion that crosses this line is betrayal.

The question is: what is the legal basis of this line? Can a Singaporean Chinese, without questioning national loyalty, acknowledge that her grandmother came from Chaozhou and be moved by her story? The frozen settlement's answer is: no. Because any positive "Chinese identity" could open a slippery slope toward "Chinese identity" in the political sense. This is a defensive, almost paranoid logic — but behind it lies a clear-eyed awareness of its own fragility.

IV. The Amplifier Effect of Deglobalization

Letters to Grandma is not an isolated incident. It is a microcosm of the systemic pressure the frozen settlement faces in an era of deglobalization.

In the heyday of globalization, Singapore's "broker" identity — translator between East and West, safe harbor for capital — provided massive external subsidies to the frozen settlement:

→ Economic growth allowed every community to benefit from expansion, temporarily shelving identity questions. Dividends are now fading.

→ American security guarantees allowed Singapore to maintain a "non-aligned" posture. Commitments are becoming transactional.

→ Regional multilateral platforms gave elites an international stage beyond ethnic politics. Frameworks are being marginalized.

These conditions are disappearing one by one. US-China decoupling forces Singaporean firms to choose; American security commitments are becoming transactional; ASEAN frameworks are being marginalized by great-power competition. External subsidies shrink; internal contradictions surface. Against this backdrop, any event that loosens the frozen settlement — a film, a social media post, an election — gets amplified into a "national security issue."

Singapore's response is not an overreaction. Given its structural position and institutional choices, it is the most rational response available. The problem is that the marginal returns of this rationality are declining. Every time nostalgia is labeled "united front work," every time online speech is blocked, institutional credibility is consumed — and the perception among some Chinese that "the state is suppressing us" is reinforced.

The frozen settlement is not collapsing. But it is entering a phase of high maintenance cost, low marginal return. Singapore needs more than stricter enforcement. It needs a serious discussion about whether, and how, the frozen settlement needs recalibration. That discussion has not yet begun. But Letters to Grandma has made it unavoidable.
