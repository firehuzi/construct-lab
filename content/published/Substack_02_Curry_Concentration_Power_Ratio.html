Fault Lines in Singapore ②

Curry Concentration & Power Ratio: The Structural Roots of the Indian Controversy

The CMIO framework's frozen settlement is cracking along the distributional dimension — and 7.6% of the population holding over 25% of cabinet seats is not a crisis, but a thermostat malfunction.

In May 2026, Tharman Shanmugaratnam, an Indian-origin Singaporean, was sworn in as Singapore's ninth President. That same month, Parliament nominated two additional Indian-origin Nominated Members of Parliament. Social media immediately erupted with Chinese-language posts complaining that "the curry concentration on the streets is too high" — accusing the Indian community of being "overrepresented" and alleging a systematic imbalance between political power and population share. In early June, the Singapore government ordered the blocking of fourteen such posts, emphasizing that "these videos do not originate from Singapore" and condemning them for undermining the multiracial model.

This was Singapore's second public opinion shock within a month, following the Letters to Grandma controversy. If the film touched the frozen settlement's vulnerability in the "emotional input" dimension, the Indian controversy exposed another fault line in the "power distribution" dimension. Together, they point to a deeper problem: the CMIO framework Singapore uses to manage its communities is bearing structural pressure from within and without — pressure that is irreversible.

I. The CMIO Framework: Thermostat of the Frozen Settlement

Singapore's racial governance centers on the CMIO classification: Chinese, Malay, Indian, Others. These four statutory boxes divide the population into identifiable, quantifiable, manageable categories. The design logic is not multiculturalist respect for difference. It is the equal suppression of every community's cultural expression, preventing any single identity from overflowing into public order.

The effectiveness of this framework depends on an implicit contract: each community receives political representation roughly proportional to its population share, in exchange for accepting limits on cultural expression. When one community's share of power systematically deviates from its population share, the fairness of this contract is questioned.

Indian senior civil servants~40%

In the early years of independence, Indian representation in the elite system was seen as a symbol of multiracialism — a deliberate political balance engineered by Lee Kuan Yew. But after six decades of frozen settlement, this "necessary balance" is increasingly perceived by some Chinese as an "unfair quota."

II. Numbers vs. Perception: How the Distributional Fault Forms

The 2026 incident is not isolated. Indian representation in the presidency, cabinet, and senior civil service has significantly exceeded their population share for years. Tharman is Singapore's third Indian-origin President. His two predecessors — Sellapan Ramanathan and S. R. Nathan — were similarly viewed by some public opinion not as "capability-first" choices but as "diversity symbols." Indian-origin ministers exceed one-quarter of the cabinet; Indian-origin senior civil servants approach 40% of management positions. These numbers are verifiable; they are public information.

But numbers do not generate emotion. What generates emotion is the gap between numbers and population share — and the two radically different interpretations of that gap.

The Official Reading
This is the result of meritocratic elite selection. Indian-origin Singaporeans perform no worse than any other community in civil service examinations, academic performance, and professional achievement. Their rising representation is the natural product of competitive capability.

The Chinese Perception
This is the result of institutional tilt. When Indian representation in decision-making far exceeds their population share, the meritocracy narrative becomes suspect. Their community is being systematically asked to "surrender" power in order to maintain the multiracial "performance."

The fourteen blocked posts expressed this perception through crude language — "curry concentration" and the like. The form is unacceptable. But their very existence proves a point: the frozen settlement suppresses channels for discussing power distribution, yet it cannot eliminate the perception itself. When institutions block discourse, emotion finds the crudest outlets.

III. Zero-Sum Game: The Amplifier of Deglobalization

In the era of high globalization, economic growth masked distributional problems. Every community benefited from expansion; debates over "who got more, who got less" were temporarily shelved. But as globalization recedes, the pie stops growing — and distributional questions surface. Economic slowdown, fiscal pressure, and employment competition make every additional share of power more sensitive.

This shares the same background as the Letters to Grandma controversy. When external subsidies shrink, internal communities become more vigilant about their "relative gains." For Chinese Singaporeans as the majority community, this "relative deprivation" does not stem from an absolute decline in living standards — Chinese Singaporeans still lead in income — but from symbolic perception: the state is "taking" something from us to compensate other groups.

This perception is not entirely unfounded in data, but it is not the full picture either. Rising Indian political representation partly stems from traditional strengths in public service (education, law, medicine) and effective mobilization by the ruling People's Action Party in Indian constituencies. Reducing this complex phenomenon to "quota" or "favoritism" is an oversimplification of causal mechanisms. But perception does not need to match causality. It only needs to exist.

IV. The Distributional Fault Line in the Frozen Settlement

The CMIO framework never institutionally addressed power distribution. It was designed for a "growth First" era, with a core assumption: as long as the economic pie keeps expanding, distribution problems can be left to time. But time does not automatically solve distribution problems. It only lets them deform in accumulation.

The Indian controversy exposes the structural weakness of the frozen settlement in the distributional dimension: it provides no transparent, regular, non-confrontational procedure for distributional negotiation among communities. When one community's power share shifts, others can only express perception through informal channels — coffee shop gossip, social media venting — channels easily hijacked by extremist voices.

The government's swift blocking of posts demonstrates that institutional enforcement remains formidable. But blocking treats symptoms, not causes. Every block risks reinforcing the perception among some Chinese that "the state is suppressing us" — accumulating energy for the next eruption. This is not Singapore's failure. It is the normal aging of the frozen settlement along the distributional dimension.

V. Not Crisis, But Constraint Relaxation

Singapore's racial system is not about to collapse. But the Indian controversy shows that the CMIO framework needs regular, transparent, institutionalized mechanisms for discussing power distribution. This does not mean ethnic quotas — a solution Singapore has long opposed. It means giving every community the perception that when "fairness" is interpreted differently, there exists a referable, non-confrontational negotiation procedure.

The essence of the frozen settlement is "maintenance in place of growth." In a world of slowing growth and rising external pressure, maintenance costs increase exponentially. The Indian controversy, like the nostalgia film, is the hum of a refrigerator with a draft at the door. It is not the prelude to explosion. It is a signal that maintenance is needed.
